Hantavirus:
A History

From an undiagnosed Korean War fever to a rare vessel-borne outbreak spanning 23 countries — the 70-year story of a virus that co-evolved with rodents for millions of years, and occasionally finds its way to us.

1950–1953
Korean War: Mystery Hemorrhagic Fever
More than 3,200 UN soldiers along the Hantaan River in Korea fell ill with an unidentified disease causing high fever, kidney failure, and hemorrhage. Mortality reached 5–10%. Military doctors called it "Korean hemorrhagic fever." The cause remained completely unknown for over two decades, spurring long-running research into rodent-borne illness.

1976
The Genus is Born — Hantaan Virus Isolated
South Korean virologist Ho Wang Lee isolated the causative agent from the striped field mouse (Apodemus agrarius) near the Hantaan River. The new genus Hantavirus was established within Bunyaviridae. HTNV causes Hemorrhagic Fever with Renal Syndrome (HFRS) — a kidney-focused disease, very different from what would later emerge in the Americas. Lee's discovery solved a 25-year medical mystery.
1982–1991
European Variants: Puumala and Seoul Viruses
Puumala virus (PUUV), carried by the bank vole, was identified in Scandinavia — common but rarely fatal. Meanwhile Seoul virus (SEOV), carried by the common brown rat, was found worldwide: the first hantavirus with a truly global distribution, hitchhiking on ships for centuries. Seoul causes mild HFRS with <1% mortality and is now considered endemic on every inhabited continent.
May–June 1993
Four Corners: A New Disease Emerges in America
Young, healthy Navajo people in the American Southwest died rapidly of acute respiratory failure. A 20-year-old man collapsed at his fiancée's funeral — she had died days earlier. CDC identified Sin Nombre virus (SNV) in deer mice. Unlike Asian strains, SNV attacked the lungs. Hantavirus Pulmonary Syndrome (HPS) was formally defined. An El Niño spring had exploded deer mouse populations, pushing rodents into homes. CFR: 38%.

1995 — The Crucial Discovery
Andes Virus: The Only Hantavirus That Spreads Person-to-Person
During an HPS cluster in southern Argentina, epidemiologists observed something unprecedented: some patients had no rodent exposure — they had caught it from a sick person. Andes virus (ANDV), carried by the long-tailed pygmy rice rat, was confirmed as the first and only hantavirus capable of human-to-human transmission. The mechanism involves close contact with an acutely ill patient. This unique property — absent in all other known hantaviruses — is what made the 2026 MV Hondius outbreak possible.

1999–2009
New World Diversity: 30+ American Strains Identified
Genomic surveillance revealed vast diversity: New York, Bayou, Black Creek Canal, Laguna Negra viruses — each co-evolved with a specific rodent host over millions of years. Scientists established that hantaviruses and their hosts co-speciated, with viral lineages as ancient as the rodent lineages themselves, some dating back 20–30 million years.
August 2012
Yosemite National Park Outbreak
Ten visitors to Yosemite's Curry Village developed HPS; three died. The NPS/CDC launched the largest hantavirus notification campaign in US history — contacting 230,000 past visitors. The "Signature Tent Cabins" had created ideal rodent nesting space between double walls. All were demolished. The outbreak showed glamping infrastructure in rodent-endemic wilderness carries real HPS risk.

2013–2025
Endemic ANDV Burden in Patagonia
Argentina and Chile report 100–200 ANDV cases annually in Patagonia's lake district. La Niña wet seasons boost rodent populations and case counts. A WHO/PAHO surveillance network tracks seasonal trends. Between June 2025 and April 2026, Argentina recorded 101 confirmed cases — the highest annual total in a decade.
April–May 2026 · ACTIVE
MV Hondius: First International Vessel-Borne ANDV Outbreak
A Dutch polar cruise ship departing Ushuaia becomes the vector for the first documented multi-country ANDV cluster. The index case — a Dutch man in his 70s who spent 4 months trekking in Patagonia — died 11 April. Human-to-human transmission spread to at least 2 further passengers. By 12 May, 23 countries across 4 continents were involved. WHO confirmed H2H transmission in DON600 (8 May 2026). Sub-national contact tracing active in Italy (4 regions), USA (8 states/cities), France, Spain, South Africa, UK, and more.
